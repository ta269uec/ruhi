The one weakness, stated plainly
To an American investor, Ruhi means nothing on first contact. That is not fatal — Vanguard, Betterment and Wealthfront all had to teach their names too — but it means the name may never travel alone until it is known. Every appearance of RUHI carries a descriptor beneath it. That is the whole fix, and it is section 03.
02
What the
name means
Layer one · soul
Ruhi means soul. Your product strips a market slice down to its essence — forget the narrative, forget the ticker, what are you paying? A valuation app named after essence is a pun that never needs explaining.
Layer two · not a finance word
No -vest, no -folio, no Capital, no Alpha. Two syllables, four letters, one hard consonant. It is spellable after hearing it once, which is the only real test.
Layer three · it's personal
Founder-named products carry conviction. You never have to say whose name it is — one line on an About screen does more brand work than any agency tagline: named for someone whose name means soul.
03
The descriptor
DECIDE
A descriptor is not a tagline. It is two or three words locked to the wordmark, in every size, forever — the thing that makes RUHI legible to someone who has never heard it. It must say the category and the angle in one breath.

My recommendation: “Price in perspective.” “Essence of price” is the truest translation of the name, but essence is a soft word in a category where people are deciding where money goes; American investors read it as wellness, not analysis. Perspective does the same job with a word that already means “relative to history” in the reader's head. Keep “the essence of price” — it is beautiful — as the opening line of your About screen, where the reader has already committed to reading.
04
The mark
DECIDE
Three directions, all built from the same geometry the product already uses: a hairline, a range, and one solid square. No illustration, no abstract swoosh — the mark should be a measuring instrument, because that is what the app is.
A · Range mark
PICK
The twenty-year range as a capped vertical line, with today's price marked low in it. It is literally the product's one screen, reduced to four strokes. The bar can sit high or low — a living mark that could animate to the market it describes.
Survives to 16px. Reads as an instrument, not a logo. Risk: resembles a fader or an I-beam at a glance.
B · Plumb line
A plumb bob: the oldest tool for finding true. Says this is what straight actually looks like — the honesty half of your brand rather than the measurement half.
Most distinctive of the three and the warmest fit for “soul”. Risk: the metaphor needs a sentence; it says gravity, not valuation.
C · Aperture
The system's own registration frame with a solid square inside it — the inner square is the price, the frame is the history it sits in. Cheapest to build and already used in the app bar.
Perfectly on-system and completely safe. Risk: generic — a square in a square is a hundred other brands.

The lockup

Do
  • Always set the descriptor with the wordmark, at one third its size, in Barlow, all caps, .2em tracking.
  • Let the mark stand alone only where the name is already on screen — app icon, favicon, loading state.
  • Keep it steel on paper, or paper on steel. Nothing else.
Don't
  • Don't set RUHI alone in marketing until the name is known — 12 to 18 months, minimum.
  • Don't translate the name in the interface (“Ruhi, meaning soul…”) — the About screen is where that lives.
  • Don't add a gradient, a rounded corner, or a second colour to the mark.

The About

This text now sits behind the About link in the app. It is the only place the name explains itself.
The essence of a price
Ruhi means soul. It is the right name for this app because a price is the only part of an investment you actually control, and almost the only part anyone ignores. Strip away the story, the ticker and the forecast, and what is left — what you paid, measured against everything that slice of the market has ever cost — is the thing that decides your return.
So Ruhi does one thing. It takes every slice of the market — the nine US size and style boxes, dividend and income, international developed and emerging, gold, silver, energy and the rest — and asks how expensive each one is today relative to its own twenty-year history. Not against each other. Against itself.
Then it says so in plain English: what you are buying, why it is priced where it is, what could go wrong, and which funds own it and at what cost. It will not tell you what to buy. It will tell you what is cheap.
Named for someone whose name means soul.
